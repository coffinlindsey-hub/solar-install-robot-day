Hand calculation against the simulation

A model should be checked against arithmetic you can do on paper. The first rows use only averages. The gap between the paper number and the simulated number is then split by where the time went.

Overall equipment effectiveness

OEE is one number made of three. Availability asks: of the time we planned to run, how much did we run? Performance asks: while running, how close to the ideal rate were we? Quality asks: how many panels were right first time? Multiply the three.

Robot and crew against a manual crew

The manual crew is a simple calculation, not a simulation: people, times a rate, times the hours worked. Public rates for manual crews disagree widely, so the published range is shown as well as the rate you set. The forklift driver is left out on both sides.

Every parameter

Each number shows its default, its unit and where it came from. sourced is a public figure with a link. assumption is a placeholder with no public figure behind it. setting is a choice about the experiment.

Why the times are distributions, not fixed numbers

  • No two cycles take the same time. If every pick took exactly 35 seconds, the robot and the person fastening would never get in each other's way, and no day would differ from the next.
  • Averages hide queues. A person who fastens in 29 seconds on average keeps up with a 39 second robot on average, but not during a run of slow panels. Waiting only shows up when times vary.
  • Rare, long events decide the day. One bad reload in seven costs more than all the good ones together. A fixed "average reload" would smear that into every pallet and hide it.
  • So each time is drawn from a triangle: a fastest, a typical and a slowest value. Rare events get a chance of happening. The model then runs many days with different random seeds and reports the average and the range.

Assumptions, all in one place

Everything below is a placeholder chosen to be plausible. None of it is measured, and none of it comes from any company.

    What this does not model

    • Fastening as anything more than one person and a time per panel. No torque checks, clips or wiring.
    • Terrain. The site is flat, and the rover never gets stuck.
    • Traffic. Robots, forklifts and people never block each other in an aisle.
    • Pallet supply. The staging area never runs out, and trucks always arrive.
    • Learning. The crew is as fast in the first hour as in the last, and on the first day as on the fortieth.
    • Weather as a forecast. Holds arrive at random, with no seasons and no link between wind and time of day.
    • Breaks and holds starting mid task. They begin when the current task ends.
    • Cost, safety incidents, and commissioning the robot on a new site.
    • Any real machine. The shapes in the 3D view are generic stand ins.
